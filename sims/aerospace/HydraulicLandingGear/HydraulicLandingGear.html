Hydraulic Landing Gear · Flight Mission

The autopilot flies the aircraft. You only move the landing gear — Extend or Retract — at the right moment. Pumps, wheel brakes and autobrake work by themselves.

Before you fly

  1. Pick one of the three scenarios on the left. Each has its own aircraft, environment, runway and landing gear, and the panel explains what it teaches.
  2. Press Start Enter. The engine-driven hydraulic pumps start automatically. When the pressure is in the green, the brakes release and the take-off roll begins.

Take-off

  1. Watch the Time Window pop-up on the flight view. Green = select now, amber = late, red = unsafe. The white line is "now".
  2. After lift-off and positive rate, select Retract G in the left panel while the window is green. The wheels are braked automatically as they fold into the wheel wells.
  3. The gear must be retracted and locked before VLO (the gear speed limit for that aircraft). If you miss the window, the flight view flashes red with a "GEAR NOT UP" alert until the gear is up and locked. If it is still down at VLO, the autopilot holds the speed there and the climb slows. If the gear is never raised, the flight can't continue and the crew returns to land: mission failed.

Landing

  1. The cruise is skipped. On the approach, wait until the speed is below VLO, then select Extend G while the window is green, so you have three greens by 1,000 ft. The autopilot keeps landing whatever you do, so a late Extend has consequences:
    • Amber — locks between 1,000 and 500 ft: the approach is unstable and the touchdown is firm, but you land.
    • Red — locks below 500 ft, the real-world limit where the "too low, gear" warning fires and a crew must go around: the autopilot presses on to a hard landing and the aircraft is grounded — mission failed.
    • Deeper red — the gear is half out or more but not locked at touchdown: the wheels touch, then the legs snap back into themselves, the wheels tear off and the aircraft drops onto its belly and grinds to a short stop in smoke.
    • Dark red — the gear is less than half out (or never extended): a belly landing, dragging down the runway in sparks.
  2. At touchdown, watch the oleo strut load in the Hydraulics box take the landing load. The autobrake stops the aircraft.

Reading the screen

  • Left panel: scenario and controls only.
  • Right panel: flight conditions (airspeed against VLO, altitude, vertical speed, pitch, squat switch, wheels) and active warnings, then the six-step sequence, the gear lights and one Hydraulics box (pressure, piston force, gear load, strut load). The flight log appears in the debrief.
  • The landing gear glows orange on the aircraft, in the close-up and in the gear hydraulics diagram while it is moving.
  • Try the wrong timing to see the consequence: extend above VLO (door overload), retract late (gear-not-up alert, speed held at VLO), never retract (return to land), extend late (unstable, firm landing), later (hard landing below 500 ft), too late (gear collapse) or never (belly landing).
  • How a pump, selector valve and actuator move the gear: Force = Pressure × Area. Different aircraft use different pressures (207 or 345 bar) and piston sizes.
  • Why a heavier gear travels more slowly, so the pilot must select it earlier.
  • Why gear has a speed limit (VLO): the airflow load on the legs and doors grows very fast with speed. Above VLO the gear can stall part-way and the doors can be bent or torn off.
  • The take-off order: lift-off → positive rate → retract. The squat switch stops the gear retracting while the aircraft is on the ground.
  • Why the wheels are braked automatically before they enter the wheel wells.
  • The up-lock holds the gear in flight; the down-lock stops it folding on touchdown. "Three greens" means every down-lock is engaged. An unlocked gear collapses; a gear that is still up means a belly landing.
  • How the oleo strut works as the suspension: nitrogen gas is the spring, oil forced through a small hole (the orifice) is the damper. Its pressure is P = F ÷ A.
  • Why a firm wet-runway landing or a short rough-strip landing needs a stronger, longer-stroke strut.
