Orbit Explorer

  1. Drag or tap the Altitude dial. Speed and period recompute from gravity every frame; there is no separate control for either.
  2. Pick ISS (LEO), GPS (MEO) or INSAT-3DR (GEO) to snap the dial to a real orbit and check the computed period against the published one. All three keep flying the whole time.
  3. Switch Orbit shape to Elliptical and drag Eccentricity to stretch the orbit. Periapsis and apoapsis are shown as a(1−e) and a(1+e).
  4. Use the Mission clock to choose how many real seconds one GEO orbit takes. It speeds every satellite up together.
  5. Turn on Earth rotation to see a ground point turning once per sidereal day, and Ground track to see where each satellite passes over the Earth.
  6. Read every result in the Numeric readout: altitude, semi-major axis, period and velocity in km, s and km/s.
  • Altitude determines speed and period — there's no independent control for either
  • GEO's altitude is derived, not chosen: solve T = 2π√(a³/GM) for a with T set to one sidereal day
  • GPS's period is a deliberate resonance with Earth's rotation, not a coincidence
  • Circular orbits are the zero-eccentricity case of a more general ellipse
  • Escape velocity is the same formula family as orbital velocity, one factor of √2 apart
  • A ground track's shape (racing band, daily loop, fixed point) is a direct readout of orbital period

Why doesn't it fall?

Gravity pulls a satellite toward Earth's centre all the time. It is falling.

Moving in a circle needs a centre-seeking force. For a satellite, gravity is that force. If the satellite moves sideways fast enough, the ground curves away beneath it as fast as it falls, so it keeps falling around the Earth instead of into it.

F = GMm / r² = m v² / r
→ v = √(GM / r) T = 2π√(a³ / GM)

Gravity depends only on distance, so the altitude fixes the speed and the period.
