AtmosLab: Two Planets

  1. Two planets, one screen. Earth and Mars sit side by side and share the same graphs. Their temperatures respond the moment you move a control.
  2. Pick a planet to edit at the top of the Controls panel, then change its distance from the Sun, albedo (how much sunlight it reflects), surface pressure and gas mix.
  3. Gas mix: drag the boundaries between the coloured segments, or type a percentage. The four gases always add up to 100%; if you type a mix that doesn't, the planet waits until you fix it.
  4. The experiment reality cannot run: press Mars's air at Earth's pressure. Mars keeps its 95% carbon dioxide but gets as much air as Earth.
  5. Presets: Earth defaults or Mars defaults load a known planet into the one you are editing; Reset custom brings back your own settings.
  6. Day and night run on a shared clock, sped up: one Earth day passes in about 3 minutes at 1×. Use 1× / 5× / 20× and Pause. The temperature graph shows a point on each planet's equator through its day.
  7. Altitude: drag the marker on the profile graph (0–60 km) to read the temperature and pressure at that height on both planets.
  8. Tap anything marked Why? (the sliders' names, the CO₂ segment, the greenhouse readout, the UV readout and both graphs) for the idea behind it.
  9. Export CSV saves the temperatures recorded during your run.
  • Distance and albedo set a planet's starting temperature: sunlight thins with the square of distance, and reflected light never warms anything.
  • Pressure beats percentage: Mars's air is almost all carbon dioxide, yet it barely warms the planet, because there is so little air.
  • Earth's greenhouse is mostly water vapour and clouds, not mostly carbon dioxide.
  • Day and night: Earth and Mars have days of almost the same length but very different temperature swings. The atmosphere is the difference.
  • UV: Mars gets dangerous UV despite weaker sunlight, because it has no ozone to filter it.
  • Vertical structure: Earth's stratosphere is warmed by ozone; Mars has no ozone and so no stratosphere.
